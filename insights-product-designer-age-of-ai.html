Product design in the age of AI, without losing the plot.

Reading notes on Gabi Duncombe's essay. The tools will keep changing; what stays is curiosity, product judgment, and the ability to say what actually changed because of your work. Illustrated, so the main ideas stick.

Source article
Becoming a Product Designer in the Age of AI (Without Losing the Plot)
Gabi Duncombe · Senior Design Manager, Premiere Pro + GenAI Video Innovation · Medium, Dec 2025
Read the original ↗

I read this because I'm in exactly the situation it describes: I came into product design sideways, I now build with AI every day, and I want to be sure the fast part isn't eating the thinking part. Below is what I'm taking from it, in my own words, with a picture for each idea I want to remember.

Tools change. Judgment doesn't.The whole essay in four words — my paraphrase of its thesis.

01Curiosity over tools

The wrong first question is "which tool should I learn?" The right one is "what problem do I actually understand?" Duncombe's point is that a designer's edge is asking sharper questions and turning messy human insight into something usable — the software is interchangeable.

The growth-mindset corollary: tie your identity to a tool and you'll feel perpetually behind. The useful habit is to pick up new workflows and play with them early enough to judge whether they fit, rather than waiting for a course.

02The blurry edges

Product, design and engineering are overlapping more. Designers can now describe a flow in plain language and prototype it in working code. Duncombe doesn't expect designers to become engineers or PMs — but as a hiring manager she does expect three things:

03Product thinking: "how does this move the needle?"

This is the section that made me want to write the whole note up. Great product designers don't only ask "does this look good?" — they ask what it changes. Product thinking is the habit of holding three lenses at once and designing in the overlap. Hover or tap each lens.

How I'm applying this

Before every crit, I'll write the three lenses as one line each at the top of the Figma page — who wants this, why it's worth it, what makes it hard to build — and a fourth line for the success signal. If I can't fill a line, that's the real problem, not the layout.

04Outcomes, not artifacts

Busyness is easy to mistake for impact. Workshops, giant Figma files and persona decks are only worth anything if something changed because of them. Duncombe gives a simple structure for telling the story of a project, and three buckets for what "impact" can mean.

05Design is messy — your portfolio just hides it

The tidy case study (research → define → explore → test → ship) is a fiction we write afterwards. Real work loops back, abandons good ideas, re-scopes when engineering says no, and changes direction after one stakeholder conversation. Duncombe calls the rest "the work around the work" — alignment, raising the bar without losing trust, turning strategy into concrete screens, deciding with incomplete information.

The line I underlined: designers de-risk investment. We make the future visible — prototypes, frames, flows — so teams can decide on something concrete instead of an argument. That's a much better description of the job than "makes screens."

06What a portfolio is actually being read for

Three things, in this order of weight: the product and UX thinking (problem, audience, constraints, trade-offs, impact — qualitative is fine); the narrative (context, the messy middle, what you learned — a story, not a slide deck); and only then the visual presentation (clean layout, intentional type, hierarchy — it signals taste and care, but it isn't the point).

07Non-traditional paths are a feature

Duncombe studied film and worked as a director and editor before product design; she now builds creative tools for video people. That isn't a coincidence — it's the move. Domain expertise from a previous life is an asset when you point it at products for people like your former self. The prompts she suggests: what apps do I use daily, what frustrates me, what would I change, and who is building tools for people like me?

Note to self

My "previous life" is four years inside an environmental data organisation. The products I understand from the inside are the ones conservation practitioners and institutional teams actually use. That's a fingerprint, not a detour — and it's the same logic behind Webbify going deep on schools and foundations first.

08Find your design fingerprint

Designers are wired differently, and each has a core set of natural strengths. Duncombe sketches five archetypes. Nobody is one of them purely — the point is to know which two or three energise you, so you choose projects, tell your story and position yourself accordingly. Drag the sliders to sketch yours.

09So how do you actually start?

You don't need permission, a title, or a degree. Start with the products you already use, the tools you can already access, and curiosity. A less conventional path can still be a distinctive one — that's the essay's closing note and the one I most needed to hear.

What I'm keeping
  • Write the three lenses + the success signal at the top of every design file before the first frame.
  • When describing work, lead with Before → After, then process. Name which bucket the impact landed in.
  • Keep one honest "what I got wrong / what I cut" paragraph in every case study.
  • Treat each new AI tool as a two-hour play session, not a course to enrol in.
  • Pick projects that use the previous-life expertise, on purpose.

These are reading notes and my own interpretation; the framing, archetypes and story structure belong to Gabi Duncombe — go read the original, it's worth the ten minutes.
